\section{Dataset Construction}

\subsection{Source Dataset}

The \textbf{IITB English-Hindi Parallel Corpus}
(\texttt{cfilt/iitb-english-hindi}; Kunchukuttan et al., 2018) provides
over 1.6 million sentence pairs spanning news, government, and web domains.
Three hundred sentence pairs were sampled from the held-out test split of
2,507 pairs, yielding stable corpus-level metric estimates with 95\%
bootstrap CI widths averaging $\approx 2.6$ BLEU points.

\subsection{Dataset Variants}

Three versions of the test set were constructed, all sharing \textbf{identical
Hindi reference translations} to keep scores directly comparable across
conditions. A per-sentence \textbf{modification tracker} verified that each
perturbation actually altered the source text (Figure~\ref{fig:modrate}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{plot0_modification_rates.png}
  \caption{Perturbation coverage per variant. Surface: 92.3\% of sentences
    altered; Distributional: 78.3\%. Both exceed the 50\% design threshold.}
  \label{fig:modrate}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Variant 1 (Original).}
Unmodified English source sentences forming the clean-condition baseline.

\paragraph{Variant 2 (Surface-Perturbed).}
Four character- and token-level operations at a \textbf{20\% per-token noise
rate}: adjacent character transposition (\emph{affected} $\to$
\emph{affecfted}), random deletion, keyboard-adjacent insertion, and character
repetition (\emph{companies} $\to$ \emph{cojmpanies}). Whole-word deletion
(3\% probability) and adjacent word-order swaps (20\% sentence-level) were
additionally applied to simulate OCR errors and fast-input noise.

\paragraph{Variant 3 (Distribution-Shifted).}
Three lexical and demographic transformations composed in sequence:

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small\setlength{\tabcolsep}{5pt}
\begin{tabular}{L{3.6cm}L{4.8cm}L{3.4cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Operation} & \textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{Example}\\
\midrule
Formal $\to$ Informal register
  & 45-pair substitution lexicon
  & \emph{subsequently} $\to$ \emph{then}\\
Hinglish code-mixing
  & 42-token lexicon, 35\% per-token rate
  & \emph{friend} $\to$ \emph{dost}\\
Gender swap (M$\to$F)
  &  bidirectional transform
  & \emph{he / John} $\to$ \emph{she / Jane}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Distributional shift operations and configuration.}
\label{tab:distrib}
\end{table}

The \textbf{bidirectional M$\to$F ; F$\to$M} gender transform creates clean minimal
pairs: every male probe sentence has a female counterpart differing only in
gender tokens.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}

\paragraph{BLEU} (Papineni et al., 2002).
Corpus-level 1--4-gram precision via SacreBLEU (\texttt{tokenize\,=\,"13a"}).
\textbf{Word-boundary matching fails on any character-level corruption}, making
BLEU the most fragile of the three metrics. Scores 0--100; 95\% bootstrap CIs
(1,000 resamples) reported throughout.

\paragraph{chrF++} (Popovic, 2015; \texttt{word\_order\,=\,2}).
Character n-gram F-score with bigram word-level precision/recall.
\textbf{Substantially more robust for Devanagari} because inflected word forms
rarely match references at the word boundary even when semantically correct.
95\% bootstrap CIs reported. Scores 0--100.

\paragraph{COMET-DA} (\texttt{Unbabel/wmt22-comet-da}).
Learned neural metric trained on WMT22 Direct Assessment judgements.
Encodes source, hypothesis, and reference jointly in XLM-R multilingual space.
\textbf{Most robust to surface variation}; operates on semantic proximity
rather than surface form. Mean and $\sigma$ from segment-level scores reported.

\paragraph{COMET-QE} (\texttt{Unbabel/wmt22-cometkiwi-da}).
Reference-free Quality Estimation scoring only (source, hypothesis) pairs.
Used exclusively for bias probes where no gold Hindi references exist.

\subsection{Inter-Model Comparison}

\textbf{IndicTrans2 achieves the highest scores} across all metrics and
conditions. NLLB-200 is a strong multilingual second. MarianMT not only
underperforms but is markedly \textbf{inconsistent}: segment-level COMET
$\sigma = 0.157$ is twice that of the other two models ($\sigma \leq 0.085$),
indicating erratic quality across sentence types rather than uniform weakness
(Figure~\ref{fig:boxdeg}, left panel).

A key inter-metric discrepancy is also present: BLEU places MarianMT
$2.5\times$ below NLLB, yet COMET places it only $1.34\times$ below.
\textbf{BLEU systematically exaggerates the performance gap} by penalising
Devanagari surface-form differences that COMET's semantic encoder absorbs
without quality penalty.

\subsection{Surface and Distributional Perturbation Effects}

The fragility hierarchy is \textbf{consistent across all three models}:
\[
  \bigl|\Delta\text{BLEU}\bigr| \;>\;
  \bigl|\Delta\text{chrF++}\bigr| \;>\;
  \bigl|\Delta\text{COMET}\bigr|
  \quad\text{for both perturbation types.}
\]
BLEU degrades 1.5--2.4$\times$ more than chrF++ and 2.0--2.5$\times$ more
than COMET under surface noise. This ordering follows from each metric's
architecture: BLEU's word-boundary precision is maximally exposed to character
corruption, chrF++ loses only affected character n-grams, and COMET's XLM-R
encoder absorbs mild surface variation with minimal semantic displacement.

Distributional shift produces \textbf{consistent, non-trivial degradation}
across all models, yet remains less damaging than surface noise in every
condition. The residual 21.7\% of unmodified sentences slightly attenuates
corpus-level drops, meaning the per-sentence effect for truly modified
sentences is proportionally larger than the reported values.

\section{Bias and Fairness Analysis}

Five probe categories of 20 sentences each were constructed for gender,
register, and code-mixing bias evaluation. COMET-QE was used throughout as
no gold Hindi references exist. The gender probe applies a \textbf{fully
unidirectional M$\to$F transform}, and significance is assessed with the
Mann-Whitney U test.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.57\linewidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plot6a_bias_cometqe.png}
    \caption{COMET-QE per category (mean $\pm\,\sigma$, $N=20$/cat.).}
    \label{fig:biasbar}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.41\linewidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plot6b_gender_parity.png}
    \caption{Gender parity: gap = 0.0015, $p = 0.213$.}
    \label{fig:gender}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Reference-free quality estimation across bias probe categories.
    Code-mixed inputs produce the \textbf{largest penalty and highest
    within-category variance}. Gender difference is not statistically
    significant.}
  \label{fig:bias}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Gender parity.}
The COMET-QE difference between male (0.8845) and female (0.8830) probe
sentences is \textbf{0.0015}, confirmed non-significant by Mann-Whitney U
($p = 0.213$, $\alpha = 0.05$). With a 100\% unidirectional transform and
$N = 20$ per category, this is a methodologically controlled null result:
\textbf{IndicTrans2 exhibits no detectable gender bias} in estimated
translation quality.

\paragraph{Code-mixing as the dominant fairness concern.}
Hinglish inputs produce a reduction of $\mathbf{-0.0298}$ relative to the
male baseline -- approximately \textbf{$20\times$ the gender gap}. The
standard deviation for code-mixed probes ($\sigma = 0.054$) is more than
three times that of any other category, reflecting high within-probe
variance: sentences whose Romanised Hindi words are covered by the model's
Devanagari vocabulary translate adequately, while out-of-vocabulary tokens
cause quality collapse. This is consistent with the \textbf{model's training
distribution being concentrated on Devanagari-script text}.

\paragraph{Register effects.}
The formal-to-informal quality gap ($\Delta = -0.0111$) is substantive
compared to the gender difference but modest relative to code-mixing,
indicating the model handles \textbf{vocabulary-level register variation
considerably better} than script-level mixing.

\paragraph{Lexical hallucination under compound corruption.}
When multiple tokens are simultaneously garbled (Example 1, $\Delta C =
-0.578$), the encoder cannot construct a coherent source representation and the
decoder produces semantically unrelated Hindi output. \textbf{BLEU assigns
0.00 while COMET assigns 0.35}, illustrating COMET's non-zero floor for
catastrophically failed translations -- a regime where BLEU's binary signal
is arguably more informative.

\paragraph{Verb-argument structure collapse.}
Word-order corruption at a constituent boundary (Example 3, $\Delta C =
-0.436$: \emph{``no that surprise the ieda''}) causes the model to mis-parse
the syntactic structure, generating a translation that \textbf{inverts the
subject-predicate relationship} in the Hindi output.

\paragraph{Residual no-op sentences.}
Example 4 contains no tokens from the distributional lexicons (D = N), so its
COMET Distrib equals COMET Orig exactly. This represents the 21.7\% of
sentences that escape distributional perturbation and \textbf{slightly
attenuate corpus-level distributional shift scores} in Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\section{Critical Discussion}

\subsection{Metric Robustness and Fragility}

\textbf{BLEU is the most fragile metric} under every condition. Its
word-boundary n-gram precision is mechanistically incompatible with Devanagari
morphology: a single corrupted input character can prevent a proper noun
transliteration from producing any matching n-gram, zeroing out all overlapping
counts. The $-32.0\%$ drop for MarianMT under surface perturbation is $1.8\times$
larger than the chrF++ drop and $2.5\times$ larger than the COMET drop for
the same condition.

\textbf{chrF++} is the best-calibrated metric for this language pair.
Its character-level operation naturally tolerates inflectional variation and
single-character perturbations, and its 95\% CIs at the evaluated sample size
are narrow enough to statistically separate adjacent conditions. It is the
recommended metric for iterative Indic MT development.

\textbf{COMET-DA} offers the highest human alignment and most stable noise
estimates, but carries two documented structural limitations: a \textbf{non-zero
score floor} ($\approx 0.30$--$0.35$) for catastrophically failed translations,
and a \textbf{length bias} that reflects model quality properties rather than
a metric artefact (Table~\ref{tab:pearson}).

\subsection{Metric Distortion Under Noise}

The direction of distortion depends on noise severity. Under \textbf{mild
surface corruption}, BLEU overestimates degradation while COMET correctly
assigns near-original scores. Under \textbf{severe multi-token corruption},
COMET underestimates degradation due to its non-zero floor. For
\textbf{distributional shift}, all metrics are consistent in direction but
corpus-level drops are attenuated by the residual no-op sentence rate.
\textbf{No single metric is universally superior}; a BLEU plus COMET
combination provides the most complete robustness picture.

\subsection{All Metrics Are Insensitive to Register}

Distributional degradation is consistently smaller than surface degradation
for every model-metric pair. None of the three metrics can determine whether a
translation appropriately matches the \textbf{register or style} of a shifted
input: if a formal and a colloquial source both produce plausible Hindi
paraphrases of the same reference, all three metrics assign identical scores.
\textbf{Human evaluation remains essential} for style-sensitive and
fairness-critical NLP applications.

\section{Conclusions}

BLEU, chrF++, and COMET-DA were evaluated across three En$\to$Hi MT models
under clean, surface-perturbed, and distribution-shifted conditions, with a
reference-free bias analysis across five probe categories.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Surface fragility hierarchy: BLEU $>$ chrF++ $>$ COMET.}
    Drops of 15--32\%, 8.5--18.3\%, and 5.6--12.9\% respectively under
    surface noise, consistent across all models and confirmed by
    non-overlapping bootstrap confidence intervals.

  \item \textbf{Distributional shift degrades all metrics measurably.}
    BLEU drops 6.7--19.8\% and COMET drops 3.1--7.6\%; surface
    perturbations remain more damaging in absolute terms throughout.

  \item \textbf{IndicTrans2 is the most robust model.}
    Highest scores and lowest segment-level variance ($\sigma = 0.079$).
    MarianMT is the most inconsistent ($\sigma = 0.157$) and sustains the
    largest degradation under both perturbation types.

  \item \textbf{No gender bias detected; code-mixing is the primary fairness
    risk.} Gender COMET-QE gap = 0.0015 ($p = 0.213$, non-significant).
    Hinglish code-mixed gap = $-0.0298$ -- $20\times$ larger -- with high
    within-category variance ($\sigma = 0.054$).

  \item \textbf{Length bias is model-intrinsic.}
    Pearson $r$ is stable across all three dataset variants within each
    model, ruling out a metric-perturbation interaction.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Recommendation.} Use \textbf{chrF++} for development-cycle
evaluation (computationally free, robust, Devanagari-appropriate) and
\textbf{COMET-DA} with segment-level $\sigma$ reporting for final evaluation.
Include BLEU for reproducibility only; never interpret it in isolation for
morphologically rich target languages.
